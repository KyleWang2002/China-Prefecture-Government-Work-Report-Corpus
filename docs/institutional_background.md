# Institutional background: what is a government work report?

This page gives only the institutional background needed to interpret the corpus.

## National and local people's congresses

China's Constitution states that the National People's Congress is the highest organ of state power (Article 57). It separately provides that local people's congresses are local organs of state power (Article 96).

The same Constitution states that local people's governments are the executive organs of local state power and are local state administrative organs (Article 105). It also states that local governments are responsible to, and report their work to, the people's congress at the corresponding level (Article 110).

Official text:
- Constitution of the People's Republic of China:  
  https://www.samr.gov.cn/zw/zfxxgk/fdzdgknr/bgt/art/2025/art_175349a7be7e4765a8bbe77b6d161232.html

## Why the report is presented to the local people's congress

The Local Organization Law provides that county-level-and-above local people's congresses hear and deliberate the work reports of the people's government, people's court, and people's procuratorate at the corresponding level.

Official source:
- National People's Congress, 2022 revision of the Local Organization Law:  
  https://www.npc.gov.cn/npc/c2/c30834/202203/t20220311_317066.html

## What the document usually contains

Municipal government work reports typically have two broad parts:

1. a retrospective section reviewing economic, social, administrative, and policy work from the previous year (or, in some transition years, a longer period); and
2. a forward-looking section setting targets, priorities, major projects, and policy tasks for the new year.

The exact organization varies by city and year. For empirical work, the important point is that the document is recurring, institutionally situated, and produced at roughly annual frequency.

## Coding convention used in this project

The project codes the report by the year in which it is delivered to the local people's congress, unless a source creates a clear naming conflict that requires manual review.

This distinction matters because a report delivered in early 2025 will usually spend substantial space reviewing 2024 while also setting out the government's 2025 targets.
